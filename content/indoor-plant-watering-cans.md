It sounds trivial, but the right watering can makes indoor plant care easier and tidier. A garden can with a wide rose spout is built for flower beds, not for squeezing water under the leaves of a pot on a bookshelf.

## What makes a good indoor watering can

### A long, narrow spout

This is the most important feature. A long spout reaches under foliage and into the back of crowded shelves, and a narrow opening gives you a gentle, controlled stream that lands on the soil instead of the leaves, crown or floor.

### The right size

- **0.5–1 litre:** ideal for a handful of small pots, succulents and windowsill herbs.
- **1–2 litres:** the sweet spot for most homes with a mix of plants.
- **Over 2 litres:** good for large collections, but heavy when full and harder to control.

### Good balance

A can that feels balanced when full is easier to pour slowly and accurately. Try lifting it with a hand on the handle and see whether it tips forward.

### Material

- **Plastic:** light, cheap and won't rust. Good for everyday use.
- **Stainless or galvanized steel:** durable and attractive enough to leave on display.
- **Copper or brass:** beautiful, but more expensive and heavier.

### Optional extras

- **A detachable rose** (sprinkler head) for gently watering seedlings.
- **A clear window or measuring marks** so you can see how much is left.
- **A lid on the fill hole** to reduce spills.

## Specialist watering tools

- **Bonsai or "gooseneck" cans:** very fine spouts for precise watering of small pots and terrariums.
- **Squeeze bottles:** handy for succulents, cacti and terrariums where you need just a little water in one spot.
- **Self-watering spikes and pots:** helpful if you travel, but no substitute for checking your plants.

## How to water indoor plants properly

![Over-watering vs under-watering](/uploads/2022/04/over-watering-vs-under-watering.jpg)

1. **Check before you water.** Push a finger into the soil. If the top 2–3 cm is dry, it's time. A moisture meter can help for large pots.
2. **Use room-temperature water.** Cold water can shock tropical roots.
3. **Water the soil, not the leaves.** Wet foliage can encourage fungal spots, especially on African violets.
4. **Water thoroughly.** Pour slowly until water runs out of the drainage hole, so the whole root ball is moistened.
5. **Empty the saucer** after about 15 minutes. Plants left standing in water develop root rot.
6. **Adjust with the seasons.** Plants need much less water in winter, when growth slows.

## Signs of over- and underwatering

**Overwatering:** yellow lower leaves, soft or mushy stems, soil that stays wet for days, fungus gnats, a sour smell.

**Underwatering:** drooping, dry and crispy leaves, soil pulling away from the pot edge, very light pot.

Overwatering is far more common indoors. When in doubt, wait a day or two.

## Bottom watering

For plants that dislike wet leaves (such as African violets) or soil that's become hard to rewet, try bottom watering. Stand the pot in a bowl of water for 20–30 minutes so the mix soaks it up from below, then let it drain.

## About tap water

Most plants are fine with tap water. If your water is very hard, or if sensitive plants such as calatheas get brown tips, try filtered water or rainwater. Letting tap water stand overnight brings it to room temperature, though it won't remove all treatment chemicals.
