Hydroponics means growing plants in water that carries all the nutrients they need, instead of soil. Roots get water, food and oxygen delivered directly, so plants often grow faster and you avoid soil mess and soil-borne pests. It's ideal for growing herbs and leafy greens indoors.

## How it works

In soil, roots search for water and nutrients. In a hydroponic system, a nutrient solution brings everything to the roots. The plant is held in place by an inert growing medium such as clay pebbles, rockwool, coco coir or a sponge plug. The three essentials are:

1. **Water with dissolved nutrients** made from a hydroponic fertilizer.
2. **Oxygen for the roots**, supplied by an air pump, a gap above the water, or by flowing water.
3. **Light**, from a window or, more reliably, a grow light.

## The main types of system

### Deep water culture (DWC)

![Deep water culture diagram](/uploads/2022/04/deep-water-culture-hydroponic.jpg)

Plants sit in net pots above a reservoir, with their roots dangling in nutrient solution. An air pump and air stone bubble oxygen into the water. Simple, cheap and great for lettuce and herbs.

### Wick system

![Wick hydroponic system](/uploads/2022/04/wick-hydroponic.jpg)

The simplest of all. A fabric wick draws nutrient solution up from a reservoir into the growing medium. No pumps or electricity. Best for small, slow-drinking plants such as herbs.

### Kratky method

A no-pump variation of deep water culture. You fill the reservoir once and let the water level drop as the plant drinks, which leaves an air gap for the upper roots. Perfect for a single head of lettuce in a jar.

### Nutrient film technique (NFT)

![Nutrient film technique channels](/uploads/2022/04/nutrient-film-technique-hydroponic.jpg)

A thin film of nutrient solution flows continuously along sloped channels past the roots, then drains back to a reservoir. Very efficient for leafy greens and used by many commercial growers.

### Ebb and flow (flood and drain)

![Ebb and flow system](/uploads/2022/04/ebb-and-flow-hydroponic.jpg)

A pump floods the grow tray with nutrient solution on a timer, then lets it drain back. Works for a wide range of plants, including larger ones.

### Countertop smart gardens

All-in-one units with a built-in light, reservoir and pump, sold with seed pods. They are the easiest way to start and are great for herbs on a kitchen counter.

## What to grow first

Start with fast, forgiving plants:

- **Lettuce and salad leaves:** ready to harvest in four to six weeks.
- **Basil, mint, parsley, chives and coriander**
- **Spinach, kale and Swiss chard**
- **Microgreens:** ready in one to three weeks.

Once you're confident, try strawberries, cherry tomatoes or chilli peppers, which need more light and support.

## Getting the basics right

- **Nutrients:** use a fertilizer made for hydroponics, not regular plant food. Follow the dosage on the label.
- **pH:** most plants absorb nutrients best between pH 5.5 and 6.5. A simple test kit or pen lets you check.
- **Water changes:** replace the solution every two to three weeks to stop salts building up.
- **Light:** 12–16 hours a day under a full-spectrum LED. See [choosing an indoor plant light](/best-indoor-plant-light/).
- **Temperature:** keep the water cool, ideally 18–22°C (65–72°F). Warm water holds less oxygen and encourages root rot.

## Common problems

- **Brown, slimy roots:** root rot from warm water or low oxygen. Add aeration and keep the reservoir out of the sun.
- **Yellowing leaves:** often a nutrient or pH issue. Test pH first.
- **Algae in the reservoir:** light is reaching the water. Use an opaque container or cover it.

## Is hydroponics worth it?

If you want fresh herbs and greens year-round in a small space, yes. A simple DIY system costs very little, and a countertop unit is a neat, low-effort option. For a warm, humid setup to go alongside it, see our guide to an [indoor greenhouse garden](/greenhouse-garden-indoors/).
